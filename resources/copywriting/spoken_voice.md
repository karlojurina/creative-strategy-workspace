# Spoken Voice - Write for the Mouth, Not the Page

A script that sounds written dies the moment someone says it out loud. Winning scripts sound SPOKEN - and the gap between the two is one of the biggest silent killers of otherwise-good ads. A written line is built for the eye: balanced clauses, clean grammar, every sentence the same temperature. A spoken line is built for the mouth: it starts mid-thought, drops words, repeats itself, breaks grammar, changes speed. Every script in this workspace gets held to the spoken standard before it ships.

The one-line test for everything below: **read the line out loud. If a real person would say it that way to a friend, keep it. If it sounds like an ad, it has a tell in it - find it and cut it to plain speech.**

## How spoken copy actually sounds

**1. Sentence length jumps around.** Real speech snaps between two-word fragments and long run-ons - the jumping IS the rhythm. A script where every sentence is the same medium length is written, not spoken. Vary it on purpose: short-short-long, then break the pattern.

**2. Contractions everywhere.** "You are" is always "you're." "It is" is always "it's." "Going to" becomes "gonna" when the avatar talks that way. Zero contractions reads as a press release.

**3. Fragments are sentences.** "Still completely clean." "No weird corners." "None." A fragment lands harder than a full sentence because it strips everything but the hit. Grammar checkers flag them; keep them.

**4. Start mid-thought and keep the mess.** Real speakers open on "So," "Okay," "Look" - and stumble, restart, repeat a word. Our CarePod winner opens "If you have a humidifier at home, let me show you something that actually blew my mind" - a person talking, not a topic sentence. Don't polish out the natural "so" and "okay"; deleting the mess is exactly what makes a script sound generated. (Don't fake stumbles either - keep the real ones, place them where the thought genuinely turns.)

**5. Repeat instead of reaching for a synonym.** Written copy fears repetition; spoken copy repeats the exact word because repetition is how a voice underlines. "Like, actual stainless steel." Say it twice when it matters.

**6. Name the concrete thing.** Numbers, body parts, brand names, exact timeframes. "This is after using it for a month - still completely clean" beats any amount of "long-lasting hygiene." Specific reads as someone who knows; vague reads as marketing.

**7. Transition by moving, not signposting.** Cut to the next thought. Use a question, a "so," an "and." Never bridge with formula phrases ("here's the thing" is banned in this workspace anyway) and never use the same bridge twice in one script.

**8. Close like a friend, not a checkout page.** "Honestly wish someone told me about this sooner." "Nothing to lose - try it for 60 days." Not "Don't wait - order yours today!"

## The tells of written-sounding copy

These are the patterns that mark a script as generated or over-polished. Most of them are already law in this workspace - now you know why:

| Tell | The fix |
|---|---|
| Em-dash cadence - dramatic clause stacked on every line | Two sentences and a period. (Em-dashes are banned here outright) |
| The balanced triplet: "soft, comfortable, and durable" | One vivid word + one concrete image, or repeat a word instead: "nothing digs in, nothing gaps, nothing to fix" |
| "It's not just X, it's Y" | Make the turn with a hard cut to a new sentence |
| Every line built on the same grammatical skeleton | Vary the shape; if three consecutive lines match, rewrite the middle one |
| Perfect grammar throughout | Fragments, dropped subjects, run-ons - the way people talk |
| Adjective stacking: "premium, ultra-soft, expertly-crafted" | Pick one, make it concrete |
| Signposted transitions: "but here's the thing", "the best part?" | Just move to the next thought |
| Self-questions nobody asked: "Honestly?", "Sound familiar?", "Crazy, right?" | Say the emphasis as words to the listener. (Question HOOKS are fine - that's a real question to the viewer) |
| Manufactured-intimacy openers: "Can I be real for a second?" | Skip the wind-up, open on the actual confession: "I've worn these same jeans every day for a month" |
| Invented hyphenated compounds: "conversion-ready", "trust-first" | If you've never heard a person say it, don't write it |
| The pristine CTA: "transform your X today!" | A friend's recommendation + risk reversal |
| Ellipsis as hesitation: "and then… it hit me" | People fill pauses with sound - "so", "I mean", a repeated word - not silence |

## The read-aloud test (run it on every script)

Read the full script out loud, at performance speed, before it goes anywhere. Three checks:

1. **Breath check.** Mark where you breathe. Evenly-spaced breaths = flat rhythm = written. Break some sentences, run others together.
2. **Stumble check.** Any line you trip over, the creator will trip over. Rewrite it the way you'd actually say it.
3. **Cringe check.** Any line that makes you feel like you're reading an ad has a tell in it. Find it in the table above and cut to plain speech.

A useful final gate: put your draft next to two or three real transcripts (your brand's winners, or top organic creators in the niche). If your draft is the cleanest one on the table, it fails.

## VoC: voice it, don't paste it

Customer language arrives WRITTEN - reviews, survey answers, Reddit comments are typed, punctuated, composed. That matters:

- **Into a static ad**: ship the verbatim untouched. Typed language on a typed surface - perfect.
- **Into a spoken script**: nobody SAYS their own review. Keep the customer's exact vocabulary (their words for the problem, the body part, the feeling - that's the gold), but re-cadence the line for the mouth.

Written source: *"I forgot I was even wearing them by lunch, no red marks at all."*
Voiced for a script: *"And guys, I literally forgot I was wearing them. Look. No red marks. None."*

Same words survived - "forgot I was wearing them," "red marks." The typed cadence didn't. The brand's `research/voice_of_customer.md` bank feeds both registers.

## Match the brand's register

Before adapting any script for a brand, listen to how that brand (and its niche) actually sounds - winning ads sound like the audience, not like advertising in general. Pull the brand's best-performing scripts (and the niche's top organic creators - the purest spoken register there is, because no agency touched it) and read them ALOUD. Note five things:

1. **Pacing** - how short, how jumpy, how fast the cuts between thoughts.
2. **Diction** - the actual recurring words. Blunt or soft? Their exact phrases.
3. **Signature moves** - structural tics the winners reuse.
4. **Address habit** - how "you" gets used: crude buddy, peer sharing a tip, expert to patient.
5. **CTA shape** - exactly how they close.

And note what NEVER appears: the words absent from every winner are as much the voice as the words present. If the niche never says "elevate," writing "elevate" breaks the register no matter how nice the sentence is. Log those in the brand's VoC bank under Anti-language.
